Definition of Accessible Web Design

Accessible web design is the process of creating websites and other online media that is accessible (people can navigate, understand and use) for individuals with different abilities.

Purpose of Accessible Web Design

The purpose of accessibility is making designs accessible for all people to interact with. We create accessible designs because we want individuals of any ability to be able to participate in and interact with society. As computers, cell phones, and other electronic devices have become an integral part of society, we need designs that make it possible for everyone to be included in our society.

Goal of Accessible Web Design

Our goal as web developers, designers, and computer scientists should be to make websites accessible for all, no matter what. One of the best ways to do this is to develop projects that already integrate accessible designs the first time around. We should be prioritizing accessibility in our original designs, not slapping it on later when someone complains about it. It is not complicated to integrate accessibility into our projects the first time around and we need to make this a universal choice.

Seven Principles of Universal Design

Here are the seven principles of Universal design From the online text Roadmap to Web Accessibility in Higher Education:

Equitable Use

Design provides the same means of use for anyone accessing resources: identical whenever possible, equivalent when not.

Flexibility in Use

Design accommodates a wide range of individual preferences and abilities. (e.g., left or right handed access and use.)

Simple & Intuitive Use

Design is easy to understand, regardless of the user’s experience, knowledge, language skills, or current concentration level.

Perceptible Information

Design communicates necessary information effectively to the user, regardless of ambient conditions or the user’s sensory abilities.

Tolerance for Error

Design minimizes hazards and the adverse consequences of accidental or unintended actions.

Low Physical Effort

Design can be used efficiently and comfortably. (e.g., online classes allow consumption of information with little eye strain and fatigue.)

Size & Space for Approach & Use

Design provides for approach, reach, manipulation, and use regardless of user’s body size, posture, or mobility. (e.g., online learning platforms allow for the use/integration of assistive devices such as a JAWS reader.)

Users of Accessible Web Design

There are many users of accessible web design, including people with disabilities, people with temporary impairments, and people with situational limitations. Here are some examples of a potential user below:

  • Visual Impairment from blindness, to low vision, to colorblindness
  • Hearing Impairment from deafness to hard of hearing
  • Motor Impairment like limited fine motor control, limited movement, paralysis, cerebral palsy, and arthritis
  • Cognitive Impairment like learning disabilities, distractibility, dyslexia, and inability to remember or focus on large amounts of information
  • Invisible Disabilities like chronic pain, mental health conditions, and neurological disorders

We need to account for all of these impairments and disabilities on our websites. Many of these impairments require separate types of fixes and we need to make sure we are accounting for all possible users. Sometimes we will design a website that makes it difficult for one of these groups to use. Nobody is perfect and these things happen, but we should make changes to our website if we realize it will cause struggles for one of these groups.

Standards for Accessible Web Design

WCAG 2.2 Standards

  • WCAG 2.2 is developed through the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) through its Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI). It is the current accessibility standard. It is organized around four main principles known as POUR:
    • Perceivable: Information must be presented in a way that all users can perceive.
    • Operable: Users must be able to operate the interface. For example, websites should support keyboard navigation, visible focus indicators, and interactions that don't require precise mouse movement.
    • Understandable: Content and navigation should be predictable and understandable.
    • Robust: Websites should work reliably in different browsers and with different technologies.
  • The WCAG standard contains three compliance levels:
    • Level A: Foundational accessibility requirements.
    • Level AA: Additional accessibility requirements addressing common accessibility barriers.
    • Level AAA: The highest level, containing additional demanding requirements.

Level AA is used as a technical benchmark for many accessibility regulations.

Regulations for Accessible Web Design

Overview of Accessibility Regulations
Regulation Who It Applies To Requirements
ADA Title II State and local governments. Government websites and mobile apps must be accessible to people with disabilities. Examples include support for screen readers and keyboard navigation.
ADA Title III Covered private businesses and places open to the public. Businesses must provide people with disabilities equal access to their goods and services. This includes online services, but the regulation does not specify a particular WCAG version.
Rehabilitation Act, Section 508 Federal government agencies. Federal websites, documents, and software must be accessible. Generally, this is associated with WCAG 2.0 Level AA.
Rehabilitation Act, Section 504 Organizations receiving federal financial assistance. People with disabilities must have equal access to federally funded programs and services. For example, students must be able to access course materials and assignments.
Colorado HB21-1110 Colorado state and local government agencies. Government websites must be accessible. The standards incorporate WCAG 2.1 Levels A and AA.
